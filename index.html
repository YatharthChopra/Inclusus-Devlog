00The problem
stated

The design challenge

Inclusus is a 2D stealth horror game about Nin, a woman who has sealed herself inside her apartment complex as the building turns into something else. Three of us are building it: Jasmine Van Bussel on narrative and level design, Alice Bellefeuille on programming, VFX and audio, and me on level design, programming and audio implementation.

The genre has a contradiction sitting inside it, and I spent most of this semester stuck in that contradiction.

Horror wants the player to see as little as possible. Our build proved that. When nine people playtested it on 27 October, the note that came back was that the level wasn't dark enough, and that the flashlight felt pointless because you could see fine without it. Alice went away and made everything past a radius around the player fall off into black. Right call for the horror.

Stealth wants the opposite. A stealth game runs on information. The player has to know where an enemy is looking and how close it is to noticing them. Take that away and the game stops being stealth and turns into guessing, which in a horror game just feels unfair.

How much can I show the player before the dark stops being dark?

Three entries. One is about an overlay I built and then deleted. One is about the order you teach things in. One is about a number that changed by 0.45 units.

01Overlays
& doubt

The sight line I built, then removed

I found out the hard way that readable and informative aren't the same thing.

We have two enemy types. The Wandering Shadow patrols and runs a five-state machine: Idle, Stalk, Inspect, Alert, Attack. The Glaring Eyes don't move. They sit in the wallpaper and alternate their gaze between two gaps, with three states: Idle, Distracted, Staring. I built both from Jasmine's write-up, and they feed into Alice's detection meter, which counts 0 to 100 in the corner of the screen.

None of that was visible in greybox. The enemies were coloured rectangles that either caught you or didn't, and in testing we kept walking into them without understanding why. So I added two things to make awareness readable.

The first was a soft vision cone: a faded wedge coming off the enemy in whatever direction it's facing, blue for the Glaring Eyes and red for the Wandering Shadow. The second was a sight line, a thin red line drawn from the enemy straight to the player whenever line of sight was clear.

It worked. I also fixed line of sight so enemies couldn't see through walls, and so a Wandering Shadow would back off instead of following Nin into a crouch space. With the noise system and the flickering wall lights that pull shadows toward them, the player could finally read what was going on.

They could read it too well.

Playing it, the cone and the sight line together were just too much. The screen was telling me the same thing three ways at once, and the second an enemy noticed me there was no doubt left about it. No moment where I wasn't sure whether I'd been seen. That uncertainty is most of what makes this kind of game tense, and I had debugged it out without noticing.

The other reason is about where the game is going. Greybox is temporary. Once the real art is in, the enemies will show where they're looking through the art, and they should do that with restraint: enough for the player to read the gaze, not so much that it's spelled out for them. A hard red line pointing at the player is the opposite of restraint. Keeping it would have meant building the levels around a signal that was going to disappear anyway.

In week 8 I removed the sight line and kept the cone.

Try it yourself

Drag the yellow block, or focus the box and use the arrow keys. The Eye alternates its gaze between two angles, the same way it does in the build. Hide behind the crate to break line of sight. Then flip the toggle and see how much the red line gives away.

What made me think this was a real decision and not just my preference: Jasmine was solving the same problem from the art side that same week, and we hadn't talked about it. She finished the Glaring Eyes sprites and gave them transparent eyelids, so the wallpaper reads as the eye's skin. Her note said the art exists to convey the eye's current vision space without needing the obtrusive vision cone.

She was planning to delete the cone. I had just deleted the line. The information was meant to move out of the debug overlay and into the art, and neither of us had said so out loud.

The cone stays for now, because the art isn't in the level yet. It's scaffolding, and it has a removal date.

What the playtest changed

The same 27 October session handed me a second readability problem that had nothing to do with overlays. Players kept getting caught by the Glaring Eyes in spots where, as the feedback put it, they realistically shouldn't have been. The cone wasn't wrong. The enemies really could see them. The cone was just enormous, and with where I'd put the Eyes there was no fair way across.

That distinction took me a while to see. The first problem was too much signal. The second was a space you could read perfectly and still not solve. So I shrank the cone and moved the Eyes instead of touching the overlay, because the overlay wasn't lying to anyone.

02Teaching
order

Teaching five things in one room

A playtest scene built to isolate mechanics, and the reorder that fixed it.

Testing stealth inside a real level is slow. If a tester gets stuck on the first corridor you learn nothing about the hiding boxes, and if they ask what a button does you've lost the data for that run. So I built a separate scene: one room, split into five stations, each teaching exactly one mechanic, with a checkpoint at each station and a restart key.

Every station has its controls written on the wall, so testers could stop asking us and we could stop ruining the test by answering. Each one has its own checkpoint, so dying sends you back three seconds instead of to the start.

I built them in the order the mechanics had been programmed in, which is not a reason for anything. Alcoves, hiding boxes, the watching Glaring Eyes, the flashlight, throwing last.

When the team ran the playground the problem showed up straight away. The watching station, the one with the Glaring Eyes, was built around throwing something to break the gaze. But nobody had been taught throwing yet, so they solved it the only way they could. They waited for a gap and sneaked through. It worked. They passed.

Then two stations later they got handed a throwing mechanic for a problem they had already beaten without it.

They got the tool after the lock it was cut for.

That's a sequencing bug, not a mechanics bug. Nothing was broken on its own. The throw worked, the Eyes worked, the station was passable. The order just made one of them look pointless, and throwing is what the later floors lean on hardest. A player who files it away as optional is in trouble by Floor 3.

I moved throwing to station three, in front of the Eyes.

The reorder dragged a few more changes along with it. I added alcoves at stations one and five, because a station that teaches something new should still let you practise the thing before it. I made the station two enemy patrol all four hiding boxes instead of camping near one, so hiding became a choice about which box instead of one scripted move. And I rewrote all the signs. The old ones described controls. The new ones describe what you're meant to be trying.

What I took out of it: teach a mechanic before the first room that can be beaten without it, not before the first room that needs it. If a player can force their way past the lock, they will, and then the key means nothing to them.

03Small
numbers

The fixes that were one number long

Most of what made the level readable wasn't a feature. It was a value, changed once, after watching someone fail.

My level design job on Inclusus is turning Jasmine's paper maps into playable greybox: Nin's apartment across five rooms, the Floor 3 hall and stairwell, Floor 1 with the lobby and storage room, doors with matching spawn points so the floors connect, and the storage room that triggers the ending. Then enemy placement from her roam zones, and tagging the cover and hiding spots.

The layout was never the hard part. The hard part was a handful of small values that decide whether a space reads as passable.

ChangeFrom → toWhy
Floor 4 crouch tunnel width1.25 → 1.7At 1.25 the tunnel was passable but felt like a snag. Players read it as a wall and turned around.
Throwables3 carried → 3s cooldownLimited ammo made testers hoard and never learn the mechanic. A cooldown lets them experiment.
Throw feedbacknone → landing ringPlayers couldn't tell where a throw had landed in the dark, so they couldn't tell why an enemy moved.
Jumpingin → removedOne fewer verb. Every jump was a player trying to solve a stealth problem with platforming.
Lobby entranceflat → recessedThe lobby had nowhere to break line of sight. The recess gives the first floor a safe beat.

The tunnel is the one I keep coming back to. 1.25 units was wide enough. I had tested it, it worked, Nin fit through crouched. But a space being passable and a space reading as passable are two different things, and only one of them shows up when the person testing already knows the answer. Watching someone walk up to that gap, look at it, and turn around is information you can't get out of the engine.

0.45 units. No new code, no new art. Probably the best half hour I spent all semester.

The level has to answer "can I fit through that?" before the player thinks to ask.

Removing jumping belongs on the same list, even though it sounds like the opposite of a small change. Every verb the player has is something they'll try when they're stuck. In a dark room where they can't read the space they were jumping, not because the level asked for it but because it was the only input they hadn't tried yet. Taking the verb away made the rest of them clearer.

04Still
open

Where this leaves the level

If there's one thing I actually work from now, it's this. In a game built on darkness every piece of information has to earn its place, and the cheapest place to put information is in the shape of the room. An overlay is borrowed against art that doesn't exist yet. A tunnel width is permanent.

What I'm still carrying:

  • The vision cone is still scaffolding. Once Jasmine's Glaring Eyes sprites are in the level, it needs to come out, and the levels need to still be solvable without it. That's the real test of whether the layouts are readable or whether the overlay has been doing the work.
  • Alice's darkness pass landed after my cone reduction. Those two changes have not been tested together, and they push in opposite directions.
  • The Wandering Shadows still lose the player too easily when she enters a hiding zone. We don't have a loop yet for what happens after detection, meaning how you lose a chase instead of just restarting. That one is mine and I haven't solved it.
  • The playground proves one mechanic at a time. It doesn't prove they combine. Next test is the real Floor 1 run, start to finish, with someone who has never seen the build.
